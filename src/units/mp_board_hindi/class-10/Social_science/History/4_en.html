The Age of Industrialisation

The Age of Industrialisation: Industrialisation refers to a process of production in which goods are manufactured on a large scale with the help of machines and factories. Industrialisation brought about major changes in production, employment, trade and people's lives. But industrialisation did not develop suddenly; rather, it was the result of a long process.

Industrial Revolution: The Industrial Revolution began in England in the latter half of the eighteenth century. The use of machines, the power of steam and the development of the factory system helped to make production faster and larger in scale. England's cotton textile industry played an important role in the development of industrialisation.

Production before industrialisation: Even before the coming of factories, goods were produced on a large scale. This is called proto-industrialisation. Merchants supplied raw materials in rural areas, and peasants and artisans produced goods within their own homes. The finished goods were sold by merchants in towns and in international markets.

The guild system: The organisations of craftsmen in the towns of Europe were called guilds. Guilds controlled the quality, price and trade of production, and also controlled the entry of new craftsmen. Because of this system, it could become difficult for merchants to organise large-scale production, so they began having goods produced in rural areas instead.

The factory system: In the late eighteenth century and through the nineteenth century, the number of factories began to increase. In factories, machines and workers were organised at a single location to carry out production. This increased the scale of production and made the division of labour more distinct.

Spinning Jenny: The Spinning Jenny, developed by James Hargreaves, speeded up the process of spinning yarn. It allowed several threads to be spun at the same time. However, some workers, especially women, feared that machines would take away their livelihood. As a result, machines were opposed in several places.

Steam engine: James Watt made important improvements to the steam engine. Steam power came to be used in factories, mines and transport. This increased the speed of production and transport, and gave a boost to industrialisation.

Cotton textile industry: In Britain, the cotton textile industry became a major centre of industrialisation. The use of new machines in spinning and weaving rapidly increased cloth production. Machine-made cloth became available cheaply and in large quantities, leading to the expansion of Britain's textile industry.

Hand labour despite machines: Even after the development of machines, many industries continued to need workers who worked by hand. Machines were expensive, and skilled, cheap labour was more useful for many kinds of work. Hand-made work also remained important for products involving fashion and special designs.

Seasonal labour: In some industries, work was not available in equal amounts throughout the year. Such industries required seasonal workers. For example, in the gas and brewing industries, as well as some agriculture-based industries, the demand for workers changed with the seasons.

The importance of advertising: Advertisements began to be used to expand the market for industrial goods. Attractive labels, posters and pictures of products encouraged people to buy goods. In this way, advertising became an important part of the modern market system and of industrial production.

Industrialisation in India: The history of industrial development in India was deeply connected to colonial rule. India already produced high-quality cotton and silk textiles. Indian textiles were in great demand abroad, but British policies harmed Indian handicrafts and weavers.

The condition of Indian weavers: The East India Company established its control over the Indian textile trade. The Company appointed gomasthas to control the weavers. Weavers were forced to sell cloth only to the Company and did not receive a fair price. This pushed many Indian weavers into economic hardship.

The impact of British goods: Machine-made cloth from Britain began entering the Indian market. This cloth was produced on a large scale and at low cost. As a result, Indian weavers and craftsmen faced stiff competition from British-manufactured goods.

The first cotton mill in India: India's first successful cotton textile mill was established in Bombay in 1854. It was associated with Cowasjee Nanabhoy Davar. After this, the number of cotton mills in Bombay and other cities began to increase, and the modern industrial working class expanded.

Indian industrial capitalists: Some Indian merchants and entrepreneurs invested in industries in India. Dwarkanath Tagore, Jamsetji Tata and other Indian entrepreneurs made important contributions to industrial development. Indian industrialists invested in textiles, iron and steel, and other industries.

Industrial workers: As factories expanded in India, the number of industrial workers grew. Many workers came from villages to cities in search of employment. They faced difficulties in finding work, and wages and working conditions were not always good.

Jobber: The intermediary who recruited workers for factories was called a jobber. The jobber brought people from his own village or region to the cities to find them work. He held influence over the workers and often acted as a go-between between factory owners and workers.

The Swadeshi movement and Indian industry: In the early twentieth century, the Swadeshi movement promoted the use of Indian goods and the boycott of foreign goods. This gave Indian industrialists and producers opportunities in the domestic market. Interest among Indians in swadeshi (indigenous) goods increased.

The First World War and Indian industry: During the First World War, imports of goods from Britain to India declined because British factories were working to meet the needs of the war. This gave Indian industries greater opportunities in the domestic market. During the war, both production and employment increased in Indian factories.

Industrialisation and workers: Industrialisation created new employment opportunities, but workers had to work long hours and wages were low in many places. Working conditions could be harsh. Gradually, workers also began organisations and movements for their rights.

The mixed impact of industrialisation: Industrialisation increased production and economic development and created new technology and employment opportunities. On the other hand, it harmed craftsmen and some traditional industries, created difficult conditions for workers, and increased overcrowding and inequality in cities. Industrialisation is therefore considered a process that had both positive and negative effects.

Conclusion: The Age of Industrialisation was an important stage of economic and social change in the modern world. This process, which began with the development of machines and factories in England, gradually reached other countries, including India. In India, colonial policies affected traditional industries, but later Indian entrepreneurs and the Swadeshi movement promoted the development of modern industries. In this way, industrialisation brought about wide-ranging changes in production, employment, trade and the shape of society.

Q1. What is meant by industrialisation?
Answer: The process by which goods began to be produced on a large scale with the help of machines and factories is called industrialisation.
Q2. What is meant by industrial production?
Answer: Producing goods on a large scale with the help of machines and modern techniques is called industrial production.
Q3. Where did the Industrial Revolution first begin?
Answer: The Industrial Revolution first began in England in the mid-18th century.
Q4. State three reasons for the Industrial Revolution in England.
Answer: (1) Abundance of coal and iron ore, (2) Availability of capital and trade, (3) Scientific inventions and technological development.
Q5. What was proto-industrialisation?
Answer: Before the coming of factories, large-scale production of goods was carried out by workers in homes in rural areas for merchants. This is called proto-industrialisation.
Q6. Where did production take place before the coming of factories?
Answer: Before the coming of factories, production mainly took place in homes, villages and small workplaces.
Q7. Who were the guilds?
Answer: Guilds were organisations of craftsmen and producers that controlled the production and sale of a particular trade or craft.
Q8. What is meant by stapler, fuller and carding?
Answer: A stapler sorted wool, a fuller cleaned and thickened cloth, and carding is the process of straightening and separating fibres.
Q9. Why was London called a finishing centre?
Answer: London was where the final preparation, dyeing, printing and sale of cloth took place, so it was called a finishing centre.
Q10. What was the Spinning Jenny?
Answer: The Spinning Jenny was a machine that allowed a single worker to spin several threads at once. It was invented by James Hargreaves in 1764.
Q11. Why did women workers in Britain attack the Spinning Jenny?
Answer: Women feared that machines would take away the livelihood of hand-spinning workers. So they opposed the Spinning Jenny.
Q12. What is the fly shuttle?
Answer: The fly shuttle was a mechanical device that increased the speed of weaving and made it possible to weave more cloth with fewer workers.
Q13. What was the importance of the steam engine in industrialisation?
Answer: The steam engine powered machines and played an important role in the development of factories, mines and transport.
Q14. What was the importance of the textile industry in the Industrial Revolution?
Answer: The textile industry was the leading industry of the Industrial Revolution. The use of machines and new technologies in it led to rapid growth in production and trade.
Q15. How did the series of inventions in the 18th century increase the efficiency of the production process in the textile industry?
Answer: Inventions such as the Spinning Jenny, the Water Frame, the Mule and the Powerloom increased the speed of spinning and weaving, and made it possible to produce more cloth in less time.
Q16. Why did some European industrialists in the 19th century prefer hand labour over machines?
Answer: Hand labour was cheap and easily available. In many industries, machines were expensive, and there was also a special demand for handmade goods.
Q17. Why did the demand for labour change on a seasonal basis in many industries?
Answer: In some industries, the demand for production changed according to the season. Therefore, more workers were needed during the busy season and fewer at other times.
Q18. Analyse the employment conditions after the 1840s.
Answer: After the 1840s, urbanisation and the expansion of railways and construction work increased employment opportunities, but workers faced low wages, long working hours and unsafe conditions.
Q19. What were the reasons for the increase in employment opportunities after 1840?
Answer: Employment opportunities increased due to the expansion of railways, roads, ports and building construction, along with growth in industrial production.
Q20. Describe the role of advertising in the age of industrialisation.
Answer: Advertisements spread information about new products to people, increased demand for them, and expanded the market.
Q21. What is the importance of advertisements in creating new consumers? / What is the importance of advertising in the business sector?
Answer: Advertisements make people familiar with new products, increase their interest and demand, and help in the sale of the product.
Q22. Which was the leading textile industry in India before industrial development?
Answer: Before industrial development, the cotton and silk textile industry was dominant in India. Indian handmade cloth was famous throughout the world.
Q23. When and why did the export trade network controlled by Indian merchants break down?
Answer: At the end of the 18th century, the export trade of Indian merchants weakened. This network broke down because of the political control and trading monopoly of European companies, especially the East India Company.
Q24. Why was the East India Company keen to increase the export of Indian textiles after the 1760s?
Answer: The Company wanted to earn greater profit because of the heavy demand for Indian textiles in Europe. Therefore, it wanted to increase the export of Indian textiles.
Q25. What system of management and control did the East India Company introduce to establish a monopoly over trade?
Answer: The Company appointed gomasthas to control the weavers, and set up a system of giving weavers advance money to buy cloth from them at a fixed price.
Q26. How did the East India Company obtain a regular supply of cotton and silk textiles from Indian weavers?
Answer: The Company gave weavers advance payments and, through gomasthas, forced them to prepare a fixed quantity of cloth and hand it over only to the Company.
Q27. What is a gomastha?
Answer: A gomastha was a paid agent of the East India Company, appointed to supervise the weavers and purchase cloth.
Q28. Write two functions of a gomastha.
Answer: (1) Giving advance money to weavers and supervising production. (2) Collecting finished cloth and delivering it to the East India Company.
Q29. What effect did British rule have on Indian weavers?
Answer: The condition of Indian weavers worsened. They were forced to sell cloth at low prices and had to face competition from British machine-made cloth.
Q30. What problems did Indian weavers face because of the arrival of cloth from Manchester in India?
Answer: Because of cheap machine-made cloth, the demand for and price of Indian handmade cloth fell. This affected the income and employment of weavers.
Q31. Why were Indian weavers unable to get good-quality raw cotton in sufficient quantity by the 1860s?
Answer: During the American Civil War, Britain began receiving larger quantities of raw cotton from India. This raised the price of cotton in India, making good-quality cotton expensive for weavers.
Q32. When and where was the first cloth mill in India established?
Answer: India's first successful cotton textile mill was established in Bombay (Mumbai) in 1854.
Q33. Who designed the first cotton textile mill?
Answer: India's first successful cotton textile mill was established by Cowasjee Nanabhoy Davar.
Q34. Why did the production of cloth mills increase greatly by the end of the 19th century?
Answer: The production of cloth mills increased due to investment by Indian industrialists, the use of machines, the expansion of the domestic market, and the increased demand during the First World War.
Q35. What was the condition of industrial workers in India?
Answer: Industrial workers faced low wages, long working hours, unsafe working conditions and uncertainty of employment.
Q36. Who were the jobbers?
Answer: Jobbers were old and trusted employees who recruited workers for factories.
Q37. How did jobbers control the workers?
Answer: Jobbers got workers jobs and held personal influence over them. They also kept watch over the workers' attendance and discipline.
Q38. Who was Jamsetjee Jeejeebhoy?
Answer: Jamsetjee Jeejeebhoy was a leading Indian industrialist. He traded with China and contributed to the development of industrial enterprises in Bombay.
Q39. Who was Dwarkanath Tagore?
Answer: Dwarkanath Tagore was a leading Indian industrialist and merchant from Bengal. He invested in various fields of trade and industry.
Q40. Why did Indian industrialists support the Swadeshi movement?
Answer: Indian industrialists wanted to promote the boycott of foreign goods and the use of Indian goods. This expanded the domestic market for their industries.
Q41. What effect did the First World War have on Indian industries?
Answer: During the First World War, British imports decreased, and Indian industries got greater opportunities to supply goods needed for the war. This led to the expansion of Indian industries.
Q42. Why did industrial production in India increase during the First World War?
Answer: Because of the war, imports coming to India from Britain decreased, and the demand for cloth, jute bags and other goods for the army increased. Therefore, the production of Indian industries increased.
Q43. What is the early history of labels in India?
Answer: In India, labels were used to identify goods and indicate their quality. Manufacturers made their products attractive by using images of Indian gods, goddesses and symbols.
Q44. Who was E.T. Paul?
Answer: E.T. Paul was a music publisher and businessman whose company was famous for publishing music books.
Q45. When did the E.T. Paul Music Company publish a music book, and what was printed on its cover page?
Answer: The E.T. Paul Music Company published a music book around 1900. Its cover used attractive images related to gods, goddesses and music.
Q46. Explain, with examples, how industrialisation was a mixed blessing.
Answer: Industrialisation led to large-scale production, employment, trade and the development of transport, but along with this, problems such as the exploitation of workers, low wages, long working hours and a decline in employment for traditional craftsmen also arose. This is why industrialisation is called a mixed blessing.
