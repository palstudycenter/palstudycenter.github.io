📘 Unit 6(B) - Refraction of Light
Q: What do you understand by refraction of light?
When a ray of light passes from one medium into another, it deviates from its original path. This phenomenon is called refraction of light.

  1. When a ray of light passes from a rarer medium into a denser medium, it bends towards the normal after refraction. (figure)
  2. When a ray of light passes from a denser medium into a rarer medium, it bends away from the normal after refraction. (figure)
Q: A ray of light passes obliquely from an optically rarer medium into an optically denser medium. What effect does this have on the speed and frequency of the ray of light? Write it. (2024)
When a ray of light passes obliquely from an optically rarer medium into an optically denser medium, the speed of the ray of light decreases while its frequency remains unchanged.
Q: Write the laws of refraction of light.
Or
Q: Write Snell's law.
There are two laws of refraction—
  1. The incident ray, the refracted ray, and the normal at the point of incidence all lie in the same plane.
  2. For a given light, the ratio of the sine of the angle of incidence to the sine of the angle of refraction is constant, and this constant is called the refractive index of the second medium with respect to the first medium. It is denoted by the Greek symbol \({}_1\mu_2\) (mu). This is called Snell's law.
Thus
$$ {}_1\mu_2 = \frac{\sin i}{\sin r} $$
where—
  • \({}_1\mu_2\) = refractive index of the second medium with respect to the first medium
  • \(i\) = angle of incidence
  • \(r\) = angle of refraction.
Q: Write the definition of refractive index.
When a ray of light passes from one medium into another, the ratio of the speeds of light in the two media is called the refractive index of the second medium with respect to the first medium. It is denoted by the Greek symbol \({}_1\mu_2\) (mu).

Thus
$$ {}_1\mu_2 = \frac{V_1}{V_2} $$
where—
  • \({}_1\mu_2\) = refractive index of the second medium with respect to the first medium
  • \(i\) = angle of incidence
  • \(r\) = angle of refraction.
Q: On which factors does the refractive index of a medium depend?
The refractive index of a medium depends on the following factors. (2022)
  1. The physical state of the medium.
  2. The colour of light in the medium.
  3. The nature of the pair of media.
Q: Stars twinkle whereas the Moon does not. Explain why.
Or
Q: Write the reason why stars twinkle. (2017)
The twinkling of stars is due to refraction. Light coming from stars gets refracted through layers of air of different refractive indices. Because the temperature of the atmosphere keeps changing rapidly, the density and refractive index of the atmospheric layers also keep changing, so the apparent position of the stars does not remain fixed but keeps changing at every instant, which makes the stars appear to twinkle.

Whereas the Moon is much closer to the Earth than the stars, so it appears much larger than the stars and a far greater number of light rays reach the Earth from it than from the stars. Hence, any change in the number of rays has no significant effect on the total light reaching the eye, so the Moon does not appear to twinkle.
Q: In summer, at noon, on open ground, trees and buildings appear to shimmer. Why?
In summer, the air close to the ground becomes hot, so the density of the different layers of air starts changing irregularly. As a result, the refractive index of the different layers also changes irregularly, so the rays coming from a tree or building get refracted through different angles before reaching the observer. Hence, the tree or building appears to shimmer.

Q: What is the principle of reversibility of light? Explain.
If a ray of light is being refracted through several media, then on reversing its path, it retraces exactly the same path in the opposite direction. This is called the principle of reversibility of light.

That is
$$ {}_1\mu_2 = \frac{1}{{}_2\mu_1} $$
where—
  • \({}_1\mu_2\) = refractive index of the second medium with respect to the first medium
  • \({}_2\mu_1\) = refractive index of the first medium with respect to the second medium
Q: Prove that \({}_a\mu_w \times {}_w\mu_g \times {}_g\mu_a = 1\)
(figure)

Suppose a ray of light AB is refracted successively through several media, the first and the last medium being the same.

Let the speeds of light in air, water, and glass be \(V_a\), \(V_w\), and \(V_g\) respectively.

At point B, the ray of light enters water from air. Therefore
$$ {}_a\mu_w = \frac{V_a}{V_w} \quad \text{……(1)} $$
At point C, the ray of light enters glass from water. Therefore
$$ {}_w\mu_g = \frac{V_w}{V_g} \quad \text{……(2)} $$
At point D, the ray of light enters air from glass. Therefore
$$ {}_g\mu_a = \frac{V_g}{V_a} \quad \text{……(3)} $$
Multiplying equations (1), (2), and (3)
$$ {}_a\mu_w \times {}_w\mu_g \times {}_g\mu_a = \frac{V_a}{V_w}\times\frac{V_w}{V_g}\times\frac{V_g}{V_a} $$
$$ \boxed{{}_a\mu_w \times {}_w\mu_g \times {}_g\mu_a = 1} $$
Q: What is the critical angle? On which factors does the critical angle depend?
When a ray of light passes from a denser medium into a rarer medium, the angle of incidence in the denser medium for which the corresponding angle of refraction in the rarer medium is \(90^\circ\) is called the critical angle. It is denoted by \(i_c\).

(figure)

Factors affecting the critical angle - The value of the critical angle depends on the following factors—
  1. On the colour of light used - The refractive index is highest for violet light. Hence, the critical angle is smallest for violet light and largest for red light.
  2. On the pair of denser and rarer media - The value of the critical angle depends on the pair of denser and rarer media. For the glass-air pair, the critical angle is about \(42^\circ\); for the water-air pair, about \(49^\circ\); and for the diamond-air pair, \(24^\circ\).
  3. On temperature - As temperature increases, the refractive index of the medium decreases. Hence, the value of the critical angle increases.
Q: Establish the relation between critical angle and refractive index.
Or
Q: Obtain the relation between the critical angle and the refractive index of the medium.
(figure)

Suppose a ray of light is passing from a denser medium (glass) into a rarer medium (air). If the critical angle in the denser medium is \(i_c\), the angle of refraction in the rarer medium is \(r = 90^\circ\).

Therefore, by Snell's law
$$ {}_{\text{d}}\mu_{\text{r}} = \frac{\sin i_c}{\sin 90^\circ} $$
$$ {}_{\text{d}}\mu_{\text{r}} = \frac{\sin i_c}{1} $$
$$ \frac{1}{\sin i_c} = \frac{1}{{}_{\text{d}}\mu_{\text{r}}} $$
$$ \boxed{\operatorname{cosec} i_c = {}_{\text{r}}\mu_{\text{d}}} $$
(where \({}_{\text{d}}\mu_{\text{r}}\) = refractive index of the rarer medium with respect to the denser medium, and \({}_{\text{r}}\mu_{\text{d}}\) = refractive index of the denser medium with respect to the rarer medium)
Q: The refractive index of diamond is considerably higher than that of ordinary glass. Is this fact used by diamond cutters?
Compared to ordinary glass (\(\mu = 1.5\) approximately), the refractive index of diamond is much higher (\(\mu = 2.42\)), so the critical angle of diamond is about \(24^\circ\). Thus, rays incident on a diamond at angles between \(24^\circ\) and \(90^\circ\) undergo total internal reflection many times, which increases the brilliance (sparkle) of the diamond.

This fact is extremely important for diamond cutters.
Q: Why does a diamond sparkle? Give the reason.
The refractive index of diamond is 2.42, for which the critical angle is very small, about \(24^\circ\). When light enters a diamond with cut facets, because the critical angle is small, it undergoes total internal reflection again and again. The light that has entered emerges only from certain points where the angle of incidence is less than the critical angle. All the rays emerge in this direction, which increases the sparkle of the diamond.
Q: What is total internal reflection? Write the necessary conditions for it.
When a ray of light passes from a denser medium into a rarer medium and the angle of incidence is greater than the critical angle, the ray of light, instead of entering the second medium, gets reflected back into the same medium. This phenomenon is called total internal reflection of light.

(figure)

Necessary conditions -
  1. The ray of light must travel from a denser medium into a rarer medium.
  2. The angle of incidence must be greater than the critical angle.
Q: Write the applications of total internal reflection.
The applications of total internal reflection are as follows—
  1. Mirage in hot regions.
  2. Mirage in cold regions.
  3. Optical fibre.
Q: What is optical fibre?
Optical fibre is a device based on total internal reflection, with the help of which a light signal can be carried over a short distance along a winding path, at the same intensity.
Q: What is a lens?
A transparent medium having at least one curved surface is called a lens. A lens converges or diverges light by refraction.
Q: How many types of lenses are there?
There are two types of lenses—
  1. Convex lens - This lens is thicker at the middle and thinner at the edges. It converges rays of light. Hence, it is also called a converging lens. (figure)
  2. Concave lens - This lens is thinner at the middle and thicker at the edges. It diverges rays of light. Hence, it is also called a diverging lens. (figure)
Q: Explain how a convex lens acts as a converging lens.
A convex lens can be thought of as being made up of a number of small prisms whose bases lie towards the central part of the lens. A prism has the property of bending rays towards its base. Hence, parallel rays incident on each prism meet at a single point after refraction through the lens.

In this way, a convex lens converges (focuses at a single point) the rays. Hence, a convex lens is called a converging lens.
Q: Explain how a concave lens acts as a diverging lens.
A concave lens can be thought of as being made up of a number of small prisms whose bases lie towards the upper side away from the central part of the lens. A prism has the property of bending rays towards its base. Hence, parallel rays incident on the prism spread out after refraction through the lens.

In this way, a concave lens diverges (spreads out) the rays. These rays appear to come from a single point. Hence, a concave lens is also called a diverging lens.
Q: How does an air bubble in water behave, and why?
An air bubble in water behaves like a concave lens because the refractive index of air is less than that of water, causing the rays of light to diverge.
Q: Define the following for a lens.
  • Principal axis - The straight line joining the centres of curvature of both surfaces of a lens is called the principal axis. It passes through the centre of the lens.
  • Optical centre - The point at the middle of the lens through which a ray passes without any bending (refraction) is called the optical centre (\(O\)).
  • First principal focus - The point on the principal axis through which incident rays must pass (in a convex lens), or towards which incident rays must be directed (in a concave lens), so that after refraction through the lens they become parallel to the principal axis, is called the first principal focus of the lens. In the figure, \(F_1\) is the first principal focus.
  • Second principal focus - The point on the principal axis where rays parallel to the principal axis meet after refraction through the lens (in a convex lens), or from which they appear to diverge after refraction (in a concave lens), is called the second principal focus of the lens. In the figure, \(F_2\) is the second principal focus. The second principal focus is also called the principal focus of the lens.
  • Focal length - The distance between the optical centre (\(O\)) of the lens and its focus (\(F\)) is called the focal length. It is denoted by the symbol \(f\).
Q: Write the rules for image formation by a convex lens.
The following are the rules for image formation by a convex lens—
  1. First rule - When a ray travelling parallel to the principal axis falls on a convex lens, after refraction it passes through the focus (\(F_2\)) on the other side of the lens.
  2. Second rule - When a ray passing through the focus (\(F_1\)) falls on the lens, after refraction it emerges parallel to the principal axis.
  3. Third rule - When a ray passes through the optical centre (\(O\)) of the lens, it does not undergo refraction, i.e., it continues in a straight line.
Image formation by a convex lens is constructed on the basis of these three rules.
Q: Describe the nature, size, and position of the image formed by convex lenses for different positions of the object.
Image formation by convex lenses for different positions of the object—
  1. When the object is at infinity - When the object is at infinity, after refraction through the convex lens, the image forms at the focus on the other side of the lens. The image is real, inverted, and highly diminished, or point-sized.
  2. When the object is beyond \(2F\) - When the object is beyond \(2F\), after refraction through the convex lens, the image forms between \(F\) and \(2F\) on the other side of the lens. The image is real, inverted, and smaller than the object.
  3. When the object is at \(2F\) - When the object is at \(2F\), after refraction through the convex lens, the image forms at \(2F\) on the other side of the lens. The image is real, inverted, and the same size as the object.
  4. When the object is between \(2F\) and \(F\) - When the object is between \(2F\) and \(F\), after refraction through the convex lens, the image forms beyond \(2F\) on the other side of the lens. The image is real, inverted, and larger than the object.
  5. When the object is beyond \(F\) - When the object is beyond \(F\), after refraction through the convex lens, the image forms at infinity on the other side of the lens. The image is real, inverted, and much larger than the object.
  6. When the object is between \(F\) and \(O\) - When the object is between \(F\) and \(O\), after refraction through the convex lens, the image forms on the same side as the object. The image is virtual, erect, and larger than the object.
Q: Write the uses of a convex lens.
The uses of a convex lens are as follows—
  1. In making magnifying glasses - It shows an enlarged image of the object.
  2. In correcting vision defects - Convex lenses are fitted in spectacles to correct hypermetropia (long-sightedness).
  3. In cameras - A convex lens is used in a camera lens to focus light at a single point.
  4. In microscopes and telescopes - Used to show objects enlarged and clearly.
  5. In projectors - Used to form an enlarged image of the object on the screen.
Q: Write the rules for image formation by a concave lens.
Its rules are as follows—
  1. First rule - When a ray travelling parallel to the principal axis arrives at the lens, after refraction it appears to be coming from the first focus (\(F_1\)) on the same side of the lens.
  2. Second rule - When a ray is directed towards the second focus (\(F_2\)) of the lens, after refraction it emerges parallel to the principal axis.
  3. Third rule - A ray that passes through the optical centre (\(O\)) of the lens does not change its direction after refraction.
Q: Describe the nature, size, and position of the image formed for an object by a concave lens.
A concave lens always forms a virtual, erect image, smaller than the object, located between the lens and its first focus (\(F_1\)).
Q: Write the uses of a concave lens.
Uses of a concave lens—
  1. In correcting vision defects - Concave lenses are used in spectacles to correct myopia (short-sightedness).
  2. Used to spread out a laser beam.
  3. Used in cameras and binoculars to control rays and obtain clear images.
  4. Used in security devices such as door peepholes to give a small, wide-angle view of an object.
  5. Used in scientific experiments to spread out rays and to measure focal length.
Q: What is meant by the power of a lens? Write its unit.
The reciprocal of the focal length of a lens, expressed in metres, is called the power of the lens. It is denoted by \(P\).

That is, the power of a lens—
$$ P = \frac{1}{f}\ (\text{in metres}) = \frac{100}{f}\ (\text{in centimetres}) $$
Its unit is the dioptre.
Sign Convention
For numerical problems involving concave and convex lenses, the Cartesian sign convention is followed for using signs in the lens formulas. According to this—

All distances are measured along the principal axis, taking the optical centre of the lens as the origin.
  • Object distance (\(u\)): Always negative (−).
  • Image distance (\(v\)): If the image is real → positive (+); if the image is virtual → negative (−).
  • Magnification (\(m\)): If the image is real → negative (−); if the image is virtual → positive (+).
  • Focal length (\(f\)): For a convex lens → positive (+); for a concave lens → negative (−).
  • Height of the object (\(O\)): Above the principal axis → positive (+); below the principal axis → negative (−).
  • Height of the image (\(I\)): Real image → negative (−); virtual image → positive (+).

Q: Due to which phenomenon does light propagate through an optical fibre?
The propagation of light through an optical fibre occurs due to the phenomenon of total internal reflection of light.
Q: Under what condition does a lens immersed in a transparent liquid become invisible?
A lens becomes invisible when the refractive index of its material becomes equal to the refractive index of the liquid.
