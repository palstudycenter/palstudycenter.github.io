Q. What is Electricity? How many types of electricity are there?
Electricity is a form of energy that is produced due to the presence or motion of charged particles (electrons).
Electricity is mainly of two types:
1. Static Electricity:
In this type, electric charges remain at rest on the surface of an object and do not flow. It is produced by friction, induction, or conduction.
2. Current Electricity:
In this type, electric charges flow continuously through a conductor. It is obtained from sources such as batteries or generators.
Q. What is Electric Charge? How many types of charges are there?
Electric charge is a property of matter due to which it produces electrical and magnetic effects or is affected by them.
Electric charge is mainly of two types:
1. Positive Charge:
It is produced due to the deficiency of electrons.
2. Negative Charge:
It is produced due to the excess of electrons.
Q. State the Law of Conservation of Charge.
According to the law of conservation of charge, the total electric charge in an isolated system always remains constant. It can neither be created nor destroyed; it can only be transferred from one body to another.
Q. What is Frictional Electricity?
When two different materials are rubbed against each other, electrons are transferred from one material to the other, resulting in the production of electric charge. The electricity produced in this way is called frictional electricity.
Q. Why does an ebonite rod acquire a negative charge when rubbed with wool?
When an ebonite rod is rubbed with wool, electrons are transferred from the wool to the ebonite rod. Due to the gain of extra electrons, the ebonite rod becomes negatively charged.
Q. Why does a glass rod acquire a positive charge when rubbed with silk?
When a glass rod is rubbed with silk, electrons are transferred from the glass rod to the silk cloth. Due to the loss of electrons, the glass rod becomes positively charged.
Question - Why does the mass of an isolated conductor decrease when it is positively charged?

Answer -

When a conductor is positively charged, some electrons leave the conductor. Therefore, its mass decreases.

Q. State Coulomb's law based on the inverse square law of Coulomb.
According to Coulomb, the force of attraction or repulsion between any two charges is directly proportional to the product of their charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them.
Suppose two charges \(Q_1\) and \(Q_2\) are situated at a distance \(r\) from each other. According to Coulomb's law, the force acting between them is given by,
$$ F \propto Q_1Q_2 $$
$$ F \propto \frac{1}{r^2} $$
Combining both relations,
$$ F \propto \frac{Q_1Q_2}{r^2} $$
$$ F = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \cdot \frac{Q_1Q_2}{r^2} $$
Here, \( \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \) is the proportionality constant.
Q. What is the value of \( \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \)?
The value of \( \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \) is \(9\times10^9\,\mathrm{N\,m^2/C^2}\).
Q. What is \( \varepsilon_0 \)? Write its value.

\(\varepsilon_0\) is called the permittivity of free space.

Its value is given by,

$$ \varepsilon_0 = 8.854\times10^{-12}\, \mathrm{C^2/(N\,m^2)} $$
Q. Two charges \(+5\,\mu C\) and \(-3\,\mu C\) are placed at some distance from each other. The force of attraction between them is \(10\,N\). Now, if the two charges are brought into contact with each other and then placed again at the same distance, what will be the magnitude and nature of the force between them?
Given:
$$ Q_1=+5\,\mu C $$ $$ Q_2=-3\,\mu C $$ $$ F_1=10\,N $$
Initially, the two charges are of opposite signs. Therefore, an attractive force acts between them.
$$ F_1=\frac{kQ_1Q_2}{r^2}\quad---(1) $$
When the two charges are brought into contact with each other, the total charge is distributed equally between them. Let the equal charge on each be \(q\).
$$ q_1=q_2=\frac{5-3}{2} $$ $$ q_1=q_2=\frac{2}{2} $$ $$ q_1=q_2=1\,\mu C $$
Now, both charges are of the same sign and have equal magnitude. Therefore, a repulsive force acts between them.
$$ F_2=\frac{kq_1q_2}{r^2}\quad---(2) $$
Dividing equation (2) by equation (1),
$$ \frac{F_2}{F_1} = \frac{q_1q_2}{|Q_1Q_2|} $$ $$ \frac{F_2}{10} = \frac{(1)(1)}{(5)(3)} $$ $$ F_2=\frac{10}{15} $$
$$ \boxed{F_2=\frac{2}{3}\,N\approx0.67\,N} $$
Hence, the force between the charges is approximately \(0.67\,N\) and it is repulsive.
Q. Four point charges \(q_A=2\mu C,\ q_B=-5\mu C,\ q_C=2\mu C\) and \(q_D=-5\mu C\) are placed at the vertices of a square \(ABCD\) of side \(10\,cm\). What is the force acting on a \(1\mu C\) charge placed at the centre of the square?
(M.P. 2025 Supplementary)
Given:
$$ q_A=q_C=+2\mu C $$ $$ q_B=q_D=-5\mu C $$ $$ q_0=+1\mu C $$ $$ a=10\,cm=0.1\,m $$
Distance of the centre \(O\) from each vertex:
$$ r=\frac{0.1}{\sqrt{2}}\,m $$
Now, by Coulomb's law:
$$ F_A=F_C= \frac{9\times10^9(2\times10^{-6})(1\times10^{-6})} {\left(\frac{0.1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^2} $$ $$ F_A=F_C=3.6\,N $$
Similarly,
$$ F_B=F_D= \frac{9\times10^9(5\times10^{-6})(1\times10^{-6})} {\left(\frac{0.1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^2} $$ $$ F_B=9\,N $$
Since the forces acting due to \(A\) and \(C\) are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction. Similarly, the forces acting due to \(B\) and \(D\) are also equal in magnitude and opposite in direction. Therefore:
Hence, the resultant force on the charge placed at the centre is:
$$ F_{\text{net}} =(F_A-F_C)+(F_B-F_D) $$ $$ F_{\text{net}} =(3.6-3.6)+(9-9) $$ $$ F_{\text{net}}=0+0 $$ $$ \boxed{F_{\text{net}}=0\,N} $$
Therefore, the resultant force on the \(1\mu C\) charge placed at the centre of the square is \(0\,N\).
Q. Two point charges \(q_A=2\mu C\) and \(q_B=-2\mu C\) are placed in vacuum at a distance of \(40\,cm\) from each other. Find the electric field intensity at the midpoint of the line joining the two charges.
Given:
$$ q_A=+2\mu C $$ $$ q_B=-2\mu C $$ $$ AB=40\,cm=0.4\,m $$
Let \(O\) be the midpoint of \(AB\). Therefore:
$$ r=AO=BO=\frac{AB}{2} $$ $$ r=\frac{0.4}{2}=0.2\,m $$
By Coulomb's law, the electric field intensity due to a point charge is:
$$ E=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q}{r^2} $$
Electric field at \(O\) due to the \(+2\mu C\) charge at \(A\):
$$ E_A= \frac{9\times10^9(2\times10^{-6})}{(0.2)^2} $$ $$ E_A=4.5\times10^5\,N/C $$
Electric field at \(O\) due to the \(-2\mu C\) charge at \(B\):
$$ E_B= \frac{9\times10^9(2\times10^{-6})}{(0.2)^2} $$ $$ E_B=4.5\times10^5\,N/C $$
The two charges have opposite signs. Therefore, the electric fields produced by both charges at the midpoint \(O\) are in the same direction. Hence, the resultant electric field is:
$$ E=E_A+E_B $$ $$ E=4.5\times10^5+4.5\times10^5 $$ $$ \boxed{E=9\times10^5\,N/C} $$
Q. What is an Electric Field?
The region around a charged body within which its electric influence can be experienced is called the electric field.
Q. What is the intensity of an electric field? Write its SI unit.
The force acting on a unit positive charge placed at a point in an electric field is called the electric field intensity at that point. It is represented by \(E\). Thus,
$$ E=\frac{F}{Q} $$
It is a vector quantity. Its SI unit is Newton per Coulomb (N/C) or Volt per metre (V/m).
Q. Derive the expression for the electric field intensity at a point due to a point charge.
Let \(Q\) be a point charge. We have to find the electric field intensity at a point \(P\), situated at a distance \(r\) from the charge. A unit positive test charge is placed at point \(P\).
$$ E=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\cdot\frac{Q 1}{r^2} $$
$$ E=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\cdot\frac{Q}{r^2} $$
This is the expression for the electric field due to a point charge obtained from Coulomb's law.
Q. What is an Electric Dipole?
If two equal and opposite charges are separated by a very small distance, the combination is called an electric dipole. The line joining the two charges is known as the dipole axis.
Q. What is Electric Dipole Moment? Write its SI unit.
The product of either charge and the distance between the two charges of an electric dipole is called the electric dipole moment. It is represented by \(P\).
Let \(AB\) be an electric dipole having charges \((-Q,+Q)\) at its ends and an effective separation of \(2l\). Therefore, its electric dipole moment is given by,
$$ P=Q\times 2l $$
It is a vector quantity and its direction is from the negative charge to the positive charge.
Its SI unit is Coulomb-metre (C·m).
Q. Derive the expression for the electric field intensity at an axial point of an electric dipole.
Consider an electric dipole \(AB\) consisting of charges \(-Q\) and \(+Q\) separated by a distance \(2l\). Let \(P\) be a point on the axial line at a distance \(r\) from the centre \(O\). We have to find the electric field intensity at point \(P\).
Assume a unit positive test charge is placed at point \(P\).
From the figure, \[ AP=r+l\] \[ BP=r-l \]
Repulsive force exerted on P due to the charge \(+Q\)
$$ F_1=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\cdot\frac{Q}{(BP)^2} $$
$$ F_1=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\cdot\frac{Q}{(r-l)^2} (Along BP) $$
Atractive force exerted on P due to the charge \(-Q\)
$$ F_2=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\cdot\frac{Q}{(AP)^2} $$
$$ F_2=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\cdot\frac{Q}{(r+l)^2} (Along PA) $$
Therefore, the re ultant electric field is
$$ E=F_1-F_2 $$
$$ E=\frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0(r-l)^2}-\frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0(r+l)^2} $$
$$ E=\frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \left[ \frac{1}{(r-l)^2}-\frac{1}{(r+l)^2} \right] $$
$$ E=\frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \left[ \frac{(r+l)^2-(r-l)^2}{(r^2-l^2)^2} \right] $$
$$ E=\frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \cdot \frac{4rl}{(r^2-l^2)^2} $$
If the dipole is very short \((l^2\ll r^2)\), then \(l^2\) may be neglected.
$$ E = \frac{Q}{4\pi \varepsilon_0} \cdot \frac{4rl}{(r^2 - 0)^2} $$
$$ E = \frac{1}{4\pi \varepsilon_0} \cdot \frac{4Qrl}{r^4} $$
$$ E = \frac{1}{4\pi \varepsilon_0} \cdot \frac{2(2Ql)}{r^3} $$
$$ E = \frac{1}{4\pi \varepsilon_0} \cdot \frac{2P}{r^3}\quad (\text{ ∵ P = 2Ql }) $$
Q. Derive the expression for the electric field intensity at an equatorial point of an electric dipole.
Consider an electric dipole \(AB\) consisting of charges \(-Q\) and \(+Q\) separated by a distance \(2l\). Let \(P\) be a point on the equatorial line at a distance \(r\) from the centre \(O\). We have to find the electric field intensity at point \(P\).
Assume that a unit positive test charge is placed at point \(P\).
\[ \cos\theta=\frac{l}{\sqrt{r^2+l^2}} \]
Electric field at point \(P\) due to charge \(+Q\):
$$ F_1=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\cdot\frac{Q}{r^2+l^2} $$
(Direction: Along \(BP\))
Electric field at point \(P\) due to charge \(-Q\):
$$ F_2=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\cdot\frac{Q}{r^2+l^2} $$
(Direction: Along \(PA\))
Since \(F_1=F_2\), their perpendicular components cancel each other, while their horizontal components add up.
Therefore, the resultant electric field is
$$ E=F_1\cos\theta+F_2\cos\theta $$
$$ E=2F_1\cos\theta $$
$$ E= 2\cdot \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \cdot \frac{Q}{r^2+l^2} \cdot \frac{l}{\sqrt{r^2+l^2}} $$
$$ E= \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \cdot \frac{2Ql}{(r^2+l^2)^{3/2}} $$
$$ E= \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \cdot \frac{P}{(r^2+l^2)^{3/2}}\quad (\text{ ∵ P = 2Ql }) $$
If the dipole is very short \((l^2\ll r^2)\), then \(l^2\) may be neglected.
$$ E= \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \cdot \frac{P}{(r^2+0)^{3/2}} $$
$$ E= \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \cdot \frac{P}{(r^2)^{3/2}} $$
$$ E= \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \cdot \frac{P}{r^3} $$
Q. Derive the expression for the torque acting on an electric dipole placed in a uniform electric field.
Consider an electric dipole \(AB\) consisting of charges \(-Q\) and \(+Q\) separated by a distance \(2l\). It is placed in a uniform electric field \(E\) making an angle \(\theta\) with the direction of the field.
Due to the electric field, a force \(F=QE\) acts on the positive charge in the direction of the field, while an equal and opposite force acts on the negative charge.
These two equal and opposite forces act along different lines of action and hence form a couple.
The torque of the couple is
Torque = One Force × Perpendicular distance between the forces
$$ \tau = QE \times BC\quad ....\text(1) $$
From the right-angled triangle \( \triangle ACB \),
$$ \sin\theta = \frac{BC}{AB} $$ $$ \sin\theta = \frac{BC}{2l} $$ $$ BC = 2l \sin\theta $$
From eq. (1)
$$ \tau = QE \times 2l \sin\theta $$
$$ \tau = (Q \times 2l) E \sin\theta $$
$$ \tau = PE \sin\theta\quad (\text{ ∵ P = 2Ql }) $$
Case I : If \(\theta = 0^\circ\) then
$$ \tau = PE \sin0^\circ\ $$ $$ \tau = PE \times 0 $$ $$ \tau_{min} = 0 $$
Case II : If \(\theta = 90^\circ\) then
$$ \tau = PE \sin90^\circ\ $$ $$ \tau = PE \times 1 $$ $$ \tau_{max} = PE $$
Q. Calculate the work done in rotating an electric dipole through an angle θ in a uniform electric field and derive the expression for it.
Consider an electric dipole \(AB\) having electric dipole moment \(P\), placed in a uniform electric field \(E\). If the dipole makes an angle \(\theta\) with the direction of the electric field, then the torque acting on it is
$$ \tau = PE\sin\theta $$
If the dipole is rotated through a small angle \(d\theta\), then the work done is
$$ dW=\tau\,d\theta $$
$$ dW=PE\sin\theta\,d\theta $$
Therefore, the work done in rotating the dipole from \(\theta=0\) to \(\theta\) is
$$ W=\int_{0}^{\theta}PE\sin\theta\,d\theta $$
$$ W=PE\int_{0}^{\theta}\sin\theta\,d\theta $$
$$ W=PE[-\cos\theta]_{0}^{\theta} $$
$$ W = - PE [ \cos\theta ]_0^\theta $$
$$ W = -PE [ \cos\theta - \cos0^\circ ] $$
$$ W=-PE[\cos\theta-1] $$
$$ W=PE(1-\cos\theta) $$
Case I : If \(\theta = 0^\circ\) then
$$ W = PE [ 1 - \cos0^\circ\ ] $$ $$ W = PE [ 1 - 1 ] $$ $$ W_{min}= 0 $$
Case II : If \(\theta = 180^\circ\) then
$$ W = PE [ 1 - \cos180^\circ\ ] $$ $$ W = PE [ 1 + 1 ] $$ $$ W_{max} = 2PE $$
Q. What is the potential energy of an electric dipole? Derive its expression.
The potential energy of an electric dipole is the energy possessed by it due to its position in a uniform electric field. It is equal to the work done in bringing the dipole from its standard position to a given position.
Consider an electric dipole \(AB\) having dipole moment \(P\), placed in a uniform electric field \(E\). If it makes an angle \(\theta\) with the field, then the torque acting on it is
$$ \tau=PE\sin\theta $$
The work done in rotating the dipole from the standard position \(\left(\theta=\frac{\pi}{2}\right)\) to an angle \(\theta\) is
$$ U = \int_{\pi/2}^{\theta} PE \sin\theta \, d\theta $$
$$ U = PE \int_{\pi/2}^{\theta} \sin\theta \, d\theta $$
$$ U = PE [-\cos\theta]_{\pi/2}^{\theta} $$
$$ U = - PE [\cos\theta]_{\pi/2}^{\theta} $$
$$ U = -PE [\cos\theta - \cos(\pi/2)] $$
$$ U = -PE [\cos\theta - 0 ] $$
$$ U = -PE \cos\theta $$
Case I : If \(\theta = 0^\circ\) then
$$ U = - PE \cos0^\circ\ $$ $$ U = - PE\times 1 $$ $$ U_{min}= - PE $$
Case II : If \(\theta = 180^\circ\) then
$$ U = - PE \cos180^\circ\ $$ $$ U = - PE [ - 1 ] $$ $$ U_{max} = PE $$
Q. What are Electric Lines of Force? Write their properties.
Electric lines of force are imaginary lines drawn in such a way that the tangent at any point on a line gives the direction of the electric field at that point.
Properties of Electric Lines of Force:
1. Electric lines of force originate from a positive charge and terminate on a negative charge.
2. The tangent drawn to an electric line of force at any point gives the direction of the electric field at that point.
3. Two electric lines of force never intersect each other.
4. Electric lines of force form open curves. They start from a positive charge and end on a negative charge; they never form closed loops.
5. Where the electric lines of force are closer together, the electric field is stronger.
6. In a uniform electric field, the electric lines of force are parallel to each other and equally spaced.
7. Electric lines of force are always perpendicular to the surface of a conductor.
Q. Why do two electric lines of force never intersect each other?
If two electric lines of force were to intersect at a point, then two different directions of the electric field would exist at that point. However, the electric field at any point has only one unique direction. Therefore, two electric lines of force can never intersect each other.
Q. What is meant by electric flux?
The total number of electric field lines passing normally through a surface placed in an electric field is called the electric flux associated with that surface. It is represented by \(\Phi\).
It is a scalar quantity.
Suppose the electric field \(E\) makes an angle \(\theta\) with the normal to a surface \(S\). Therefore, the electric flux associated with surface \(S\) is:
$$ \Phi = ES\cos\theta $$
Case I: If the surface is parallel to the direction of the electric field \((\theta=90^\circ)\), then
$$ \Phi = ES\cos90^\circ $$ $$ \Phi = ES\times0 $$ $$ \Phi_{\min}=0 $$
Case II: If the surface is perpendicular to the direction of the electric field \((\theta=0^\circ)\), then
$$ \Phi = ES\cos0^\circ = ES $$ $$ \Phi = ES\times1 $$ $$ \Phi_{\max}=ES $$
Q. State and prove Gauss's Theorem.
According to Gauss's Theorem, the total electric flux passing through any closed surface is equal to \(\frac{1}{\varepsilon_0}\) times the total charge enclosed within the surface.
$$ \Phi=\frac{Q}{\varepsilon_0} $$
Proof
Consider a point charge \(Q\) placed at point \(O\) inside a closed surface \(S\). The electric field at a point \(P\), situated at a distance \(r\) from the charge, is
$$ E=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\cdot\frac{Q}{r^2} $$
Now, the electric flux through a small surface element \(ds\) is
$$ d\Phi=E\cos\theta\,ds $$
$$ d\Phi= \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \cdot \frac{Q}{r^2} \cos\theta\,ds $$
$$ d\Phi= \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \,d\omega \qquad \left( \because\; d\omega= \frac{ds\cos\theta}{r^2} \right) $$
Integrating over the entire closed surface,
$$ \Phi=\int d\Phi $$
$$ \Phi= \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \int d\omega $$
$$ \Phi= \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \times4\pi $$
$$ \Phi= \frac{Q}{\varepsilon_0} $$
Hence, Gauss's Theorem is proved.
Q. What is a Gaussian surface?
A Gaussian surface is an imaginary closed surface chosen in an electric field for applying Gauss's theorem.
Q. Using Gauss's theorem, derive the expression for the electric field intensity at a point due to a point charge.

Answer:

Suppose a charge \(Q\) is placed at point O. We have to find the electric field intensity at a point P, which is at a distance \(r\) from O. For this, we imagine a sphere with O as its center and \(OP=r\) as its radius. This imaginary sphere is called a Gaussian surface. The electric flux associated with this Gaussian surface is given by:

$$ \Phi = ES\cos 0^\circ $$
$$ \Phi = E(4\pi r^2)\times 1 $$
$$ \Phi = E(4\pi r^2) \quad \text{----(1)} $$

Since the total charge enclosed within the Gaussian surface is \(Q\), therefore, according to Gauss's theorem:

$$ \Phi = \frac{Q}{\varepsilon_0} \quad \text{----(2)} $$

From equations (1) and (2):

$$ E(4\pi r^2)=\frac{Q}{\varepsilon_0} $$
$$ \boxed{E=\frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2}} $$

Q. Using Gauss's theorem, find the electric field intensity due to a charged hollow sphere in the following cases and draw a graph of electric field intensity with distance:
(i) Outside the sphere
(ii) On the surface of the sphere
(iii) Inside the sphere

Consider a hollow sphere with centre O and radius \(r\). When a charge \(Q\) is given to the sphere, the charge spreads uniformly over its outer surface. We have to find the electric field intensity in the following cases.

(i) Outside the sphere:

Suppose a point P is located outside the sphere at a distance \(x\) from its centre. Taking O as the centre and OP = \(x\) as the radius, we imagine a spherical surface called a Gaussian surface. The electric flux associated with this Gaussian surface is:

$$ \Phi = ES\cos 0^\circ $$
$$ \Phi = E(4\pi x^2)\times 1 $$
$$ \Phi = E(4\pi x^2) \quad \text{----(1)} $$

Since the total charge enclosed within the Gaussian surface is \(Q\), according to Gauss's theorem:

$$ \Phi = \frac{Q}{\varepsilon_0} \quad \text{----(2)} $$

From equations (1) and (2):

$$ E(4\pi x^2)=\frac{Q}{\varepsilon_0} $$
$$ \boxed{E=\frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0x^2}} \quad \text{----(3)} $$

(ii) On the surface of the sphere:

For a point on the surface of the sphere, \(x=r\). Substituting this value in equation (3), we get:

$$ \boxed{E=\frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2}} $$

(iii) Inside the sphere:

Suppose a point P is located inside the sphere at a distance \(x\) from its centre. Taking O as the centre and OP = \(x\) as the radius, we imagine a spherical Gaussian surface.

$$ \Phi = ES\cos 0^\circ $$
$$ \Phi = E(4\pi x^2)\times 1 $$
$$ \Phi = E(4\pi x^2) \quad \text{----(4)} $$

Since the entire charge of the hollow sphere is located on its surface, there is no charge enclosed within the Gaussian surface taken inside the sphere. Therefore, according to Gauss's theorem:

$$ \Phi=\frac{0}{\varepsilon_0}=0 \quad \text{----(5)} $$

From equations (4) and (5):

$$ E(4\pi x^2)=0 $$
$$ \boxed{E=0} $$
