Chemical kinetics
What is Chemical Kinetics?

The branch of chemistry which deals with the study of the rate of different chemical reactions is called Chemical Kinetics.

What is meant by Rate of Reaction?

The change in concentration of reactants or products per unit time is called the rate of reaction.

General Reaction:

\[ A+B \rightarrow C+D \]

The rate of reaction is expressed as:

\[ r=-\frac{d[A]}{dt} =-\frac{d[B]}{dt} =\frac{d[C]}{dt} =\frac{d[D]}{dt} \]

Unit of Rate of Reaction:

\[ mol\,L^{-1}s^{-1} \]

Factors Affecting the Rate of Reaction:

  1. Concentration of Reactants: Increasing the concentration of reactants increases the reaction rate.
  2. Temperature: Increasing temperature increases the rate of reaction.
  3. Catalyst: A positive catalyst increases the reaction rate, whereas a negative catalyst decreases it.
  4. Nature of Reactants: Reactions involving simple molecules generally occur faster because fewer bonds need to be broken.
  5. Effect of Light: Some reactions proceed faster in the presence of light.
What is Rate Determining Step?

In a complex reaction, the slowest step that controls the overall rate of the reaction is called the Rate Determining Step (RDS).

The overall rate of a complex reaction is equal to the rate of its slowest step.

Therefore, the reaction rate mainly depends upon the rate determining step.

What is Temperature Coefficient?

The factor by which the rate of a chemical reaction increases when the temperature is raised by 10°C is called the Temperature Coefficient.

It is generally represented by γ (gamma).

Mathematically,

\[ \gamma=\frac{k_{(T+10)}}{k_T} \]

where, \(k_T\) = rate constant at temperature \(T\), and \(k_{(T+10)}\) = rate constant at temperature \((T+10)^\circ C\).

For most chemical reactions, the value of the temperature coefficient lies between 2 and 3.

What is Rate Law or Rate Equation?

Guldberg and Waage proposed the Law of Active Mass.

According to this law, the rate of a chemical reaction is directly proportional to the product of the active masses (concentrations) of the reactants.

General Reaction:

\[ aA+bB \rightarrow Products \]

Rate Equation:

\[ r=K[A]^a[B]^b \]

where, \(K\) = rate constant, and \([A]\), \([B]\) are the molar concentrations of reactants.

What is Rate Constant?

The rate of a chemical reaction when the concentration of each reactant is unity is called the Rate Constant.

Thus, when the concentration of every reactant becomes one unit, the reaction rate becomes equal to the rate constant.

Formula:

\[ r=K[A]^a[B]^b \]

where,

  • \(r\) = Rate of reaction
  • \(K\) = Rate constant
  • \([A]\), \([B]\) = Molar concentration of reactants
  • \(a\), \(b\) = Powers of concentration terms

If

\[ [A]=[B]=1 \]

then,

\[ r=K \]

Hence, the value of the rate constant is equal to the reaction rate.

Note: The unit of the rate constant depends upon the order of the reaction.

What is Molecularity? Give examples.

The number of reactant molecules taking part in an elementary reaction is called the Molecularity of the reaction.

Molecularity represents the number of molecules participating in the elementary step of a reaction.

Examples:

  1. Unimolecular Reaction:
    \[ O_2F_2 \rightarrow O_2+F_2 \]

  2. Bimolecular Reaction:
    \[ 2HI \rightarrow H_2+I_2 \]

  3. Termolecular Reaction:
    \[ 2NO+O_2 \rightarrow 2NO_2 \]

Characteristics:

  • Molecularity is always a whole number.
  • It can never be zero or fractional.
What is Order of Reaction? Explain with example.

The sum of the powers of concentration terms in the rate equation is called the Order of Reaction.

Consider the general reaction:

\[ aA+bB \rightarrow Products \]

Rate equation:

\[ r=K[A]^a[B]^b \]

Therefore,

\[ \text{Order}=a+b \]

Characteristics:

  • The order may be zero, whole number or fractional.
  • It is determined experimentally.
  • It cannot be predicted from the balanced chemical equation alone.

Types of Order of Reaction:

  1. Zero Order Reaction
  2. First Order Reaction
  3. Second Order Reaction
  4. Third Order Reaction

What is a Zero Order Reaction?

A reaction whose rate is independent of the concentration of the reactant is called a Zero Order Reaction.

The rate equation for a zero order reaction is:

\[ r=K[A]^0 \]

Therefore,

\[ r=K \]

where, r = Rate of reaction
K = Rate constant

Thus, changing the concentration of the reactant does not affect the reaction rate.

Examples:

  1. Thermal decomposition of NH3 on a catalyst surface.
  2. Thermal decomposition of HI on the surface of gold (Au).
  3. Thermal decomposition of N2O in the presence of a catalyst.
What is a First Order Reaction? Give an example.

A reaction whose rate is directly proportional to the concentration of one reactant is called a First Order Reaction.

The rate equation is:

\[ r=K[A]^1 \]

or

\[ r=K[A] \]

where, [A] = Concentration of reactant A
K = Rate constant

Example:

Decomposition of ammonium nitrite:

\[ NH_4NO_2 \rightarrow N_2+2H_2O \]

Since the reaction rate depends only on the concentration of ammonium nitrite, it is a first order reaction.

What is a Pseudo First Order Reaction? Explain with an example.

A reaction that appears to be a second order reaction but actually behaves as a first order reaction is called a Pseudo First Order Reaction.

In such reactions, two reactants participate, but one reactant is taken in a very large excess. Therefore, its concentration remains nearly constant during the reaction.

Hence, the reaction rate depends only on the concentration of the other reactant.

Example:

\[ CH_3COOC_2H_5 + H_2O \rightarrow CH_3COOH + C_2H_5OH \]

Since water is present in large excess, its concentration remains practically constant.

Therefore, the rate equation becomes:

\[ r=K[CH_3COOC_2H_5] \]

Thus, although the reaction involves two reactants, it behaves as a first order reaction and is called a Pseudo First Order Reaction.

Derive the Integrated Rate Equation for a First Order Reaction.

Definition:
A reaction whose rate is directly proportional to the concentration of one reactant is called a First Order Reaction.

Integrated Rate Equation:

Consider the reaction:

\[ A \rightarrow Product \]

Initial concentration of A = a

After time t, concentration of A = (a - x)

Therefore, the rate of reaction is

\[ r=\frac{dx}{dt} \]

According to the rate law,

\[ r=K[A] \]

\[ \frac{dx}{dt}=K(a-x) \]

Separating the variables,

\[ \frac{dx}{a-x}=Kdt \]

Integrating both sides,

\[ \int\frac{dx}{a-x}=\int Kdt \]

\[ -\ln(a-x)=Kt+C \]

where C is the integration constant.

At t = 0, x = 0

\[ -\ln a=C \]

Substituting the value of C,

\[ -\ln(a-x)=Kt-\ln a \]

\[ Kt=\ln a-\ln(a-x) \]

\[ Kt=\ln\left(\frac{a}{a-x}\right) \]

Therefore,

\[ K=\frac{1}{t}\ln\left(\frac{a}{a-x}\right) \]

Converting into common logarithm,

\[ K=\frac{2.303}{t}\log_{10}\left(\frac{a}{a-x}\right) \]

This is the Integrated Rate Equation for a First Order Reaction.

What is Half-Life? Derive the expression for the Half-Life of a First Order Reaction.

Definition:
The time required for the concentration of a reactant to become half of its initial value is called the Half-Life (t1/2) of the reaction.

For a first order reaction, the integrated rate equation is:

\[ K=\frac{2.303}{t}\log_{10}\frac{a}{a-x} \]

Therefore,

\[ t=\frac{2.303}{K}\log_{10}\frac{a}{a-x} \]

For half-life,

\[ t=t_{1/2},\qquad x=\frac{a}{2} \]

Substituting these values,

\[ t_{1/2} = \frac{2.303}{K} \log_{10} \frac{a}{a-\frac{a}{2}} \]

\[ t_{1/2} = \frac{2.303}{K} \log_{10}2 \]

Since,

\[ \log_{10}2=0.3010 \]

\[ t_{1/2} = \frac{2.303\times0.3010}{K} \]

\[ \boxed{t_{1/2}=\frac{0.693}{K}} \]

Hence, the half-life of a first order reaction depends only on the rate constant and is independent of the initial concentration.

A first order reaction is 90% complete in 50 minutes. Calculate its half-life.

Solution:

Given,

\[ t=50\ \text{min} \]

Assume initial concentration,

\[ a=100 \]

Since the reaction is 90% complete,

\[ x=90 \]

Therefore,

\[ a-x=10 \]

Using the first order rate equation,

\[ K=\frac{2.303}{50}\log_{10}\frac{100}{10} \]

\[ K=\frac{2.303}{50} \]

Now,

\[ t_{1/2}=\frac{0.693}{K} \]

\[ t_{1/2} = \frac{0.693}{2.303/50} \]

\[ t_{1/2} = \frac{0.693\times50}{2.303} \]

\[ \boxed{t_{1/2}=15\ \text{minutes}} \]

The half-life of a first order reaction is 2 minutes. Find the time required for the concentration to become 25% of its initial value.

Solution:

\[ t_{1/2}=\frac{0.693}{K} \]

\[ K=\frac{0.693}{2} \]

Let the initial concentration be

\[ a=100 \]

Remaining concentration,

\[ a-x=25 \]

Using,

\[ t=\frac{2.303}{K}\log_{10}\frac{a}{a-x} \]

\[ t=\frac{2.303}{0.693/2}\log_{10}\frac{100}{25} \]

\[ t=\frac{2.303\times2}{0.693}\log_{10}(4) \]

\[ t=4\ \text{minutes} \]

Answer: \[ \boxed{4\ \text{minutes}} \]

Derive the time required for a first order reaction to complete three-fourths of the reaction.

Let the initial concentration be a.

After time t,

\[ x=\frac{3a}{4} \]

Remaining concentration,

\[ a-x=\frac{a}{4} \]

Using the first order rate equation,

\[ K=\frac{2.303}{t} \log_{10} \left(\frac{a}{a/4}\right) \]

\[ K=\frac{2.303}{t}\log_{10}(4) \]

\[ K=\frac{2.303}{t}(0.6020) \]

\[ \boxed{t=\frac{1.386}{K}} \]

Hence, the time required depends only on the rate constant and is independent of the initial concentration.

Question: State the Arrhenius equation and explain its applications.

Answer:

Arrhenius Equation:

The Swedish scientist Arrhenius proposed an equation that explains the relationship between the rate constant and temperature. This equation is known as the Arrhenius equation.

\[ K=Ae^{-\frac{E_a}{RT}} \]

Where,

  • K = Rate constant
  • A = Frequency factor (Arrhenius constant)
  • Ea = Activation energy
  • R = Universal gas constant
  • T = Absolute temperature (Kelvin)

Applications of the Arrhenius Equation:

  1. It is used to determine the activation energy of a chemical reaction.
  2. It explains the effect of temperature on the rate of a chemical reaction.
  3. It establishes the relationship between the rate constant (K) and temperature.
  4. It helps explain why the reaction rate increases with an increase in temperature.

Thus, the Arrhenius equation plays an important role in understanding the effect of temperature on chemical reaction rates.
