📘 Chapter — Carboxylic Acids (Fatty Acids)
Q: What is the carboxylic group?

The (–COOH) group is called the carboxylic group.

Q: What are carboxylic acids?

Organic compounds containing the (–COOH) group are called carboxylic acids. These compounds show the properties of acids.

Carboxylic acids are of two types.

1. Aliphatic acids

In these, the –COOH group is attached to an alkyl (R) group. For example —

\( R\text{-}COOH \)

2. Aromatic acids

In these, the –COOH group is attached to an aryl (Ar) group. For example —

\( Ar\text{-}COOH \)
Q: What are fatty acids?

Aliphatic monocarboxylic acids are called fatty acids.

Q: Why are aliphatic monocarboxylic acids called fatty acids?

Higher acids obtained from fats, such as stearic and palmitic acid, contain only one carboxylic group, so all monocarboxylic acids are collectively called fatty acids.

Q: Explain the nomenclature of monocarboxylic acids.

1. In the common name system

In this system, '-ic' is added at the end of the main name, followed by the word 'acid'. The first four members are named after the sources from which they were obtained. For example —

  • HCOOH → obtained from ants. Hence it was named formic acid, based on the Latin word 'formica'.
  • CH3COOH → obtained from vinegar. Hence it was named acetic acid, based on the Latin word 'acetum'.

2. In IUPAC nomenclature

The following steps are followed in IUPAC nomenclature —

  1. Select the longest carbon chain that contains the –COOH group.
  2. The chain is numbered from the end that gives the –COOH group the lowest possible locant.
  3. The chain is named and the final -e is dropped and -oic is added.
  4. If more than one –COOH group is present, the words dioic, trioic, tetraoic, etc. are used.
Note: In the source notes this step list said "–OH group" both times (which is alcohol nomenclature terminology); since this paragraph is about the nomenclature of carboxylic acids (–COOH group), it has been corrected above to "–COOH group" to match the context.

Example:

\[ CH_3COOH \rightarrow \text{methanoic acid} \] \[ C_2H_5COOH \rightarrow \text{ethanoic acid} \]

Q: What types of isomerism are found in monocarboxylic acids?

Monocarboxylic acids exhibit the following types of isomerism.

1. Chain isomerism

When monocarboxylic acids with the same molecular formula exist as different types of carbon chains (straight or branched), it is called chain isomerism. Example —

\[ CH_3\text{-}CH_2\text{-}CH_2\text{-}COOH \quad \text{and} \quad (CH_3)_2CH\text{-}COOH \]

2. Functional isomerism

When compounds with the same molecular formula display different functional groups, it is called functional isomerism. Example —

\[ CH_3\text{-}CH_2\text{-}COOH \quad \text{and} \quad CH_3\text{-}COO\text{-}CH_3 \]

Q: Write the general methods of preparation of monocarboxylic acids.
Note: Some equations in the source do not explicitly show water (H₂O) as a by-product; where it was absent in the source, the equation has been left exactly as is (without adding it).

The general methods of preparation of monocarboxylic acids are as follows.

1. By oxidation of alcohols with acidic KMnO₄

\[ H\text{-}CH_2\text{-}OH + 2[O] \xrightarrow{KMnO_4} H\text{-}COOH \] \[ CH_3\text{-}CH_2\text{-}OH + 2[O] \xrightarrow{KMnO_4} CH_3COOH \]

2. By oxidation of aldehydes with acidic KMnO₄

\[ H\text{-}CHO + [O] \xrightarrow{KMnO_4} H\text{-}COOH \] \[ CH_3\text{-}CHO + [O] \xrightarrow{KMnO_4} CH_3COOH \]

3. By hydrolysis of alkyl cyanides

Hydrolysis of alkyl cyanides with dilute acid or alkali gives fatty acids.

\[ CH_3CN + 2H_2O \xrightarrow{\text{dil. acid}} CH_3COOH + NH_3 \]

4. By hydrolysis of esters

Heating an ester with a mineral acid or alkali gives fatty acids.

\[ CH_3COOC_2H_5 + H_2O \xrightarrow{\text{dil. } HCl} CH_3COOH + C_2H_5OH \]

5. By action of nitrous acid on acid amides

\[ CH_3CONH_2 + HNO_2 \rightarrow CH_3COOH + N_2 + H_2O \]

6. By hydrolysis of acid amides with alkali or mineral acids

\[ CH_3CONH_2 + H_2O \xrightarrow{\text{dil. acid}} CH_3COOH + NH_3 \]
Q: Write the physical properties of monocarboxylic acids.

The physical properties of monocarboxylic acids are as follows.

1. State and odour

  • The first three aliphatic acids are colourless liquids with a pungent smell.
  • The next six members (C4 to C9) are oily liquids with a rancid-butter-like smell.
  • Higher members (C10 and above) are odourless, wax-like solids.
  • Aromatic acids (such as benzoic acid) are colourless, odourless crystalline solids.

2. Solubility

  • The first four members are highly soluble in water.
  • Their solubility in water decreases as the molecular mass increases.
  • All of them dissolve readily in organic solvents such as alcohol and ether.

3. Boiling point

The boiling points of monocarboxylic acids are higher than those of other compounds of similar molecular mass (such as aldehydes, ketones, and alcohols).

Q: Why is the boiling point of monocarboxylic acids higher than other compounds of similar molecular mass such as aldehydes, ketones, and alcohols?

Carboxylic acids form dimers due to hydrogen bonding, which requires more energy to separate the molecules.

For this reason, the boiling point of monocarboxylic acids is higher than that of other compounds of similar molecular mass such as aldehydes, ketones, and alcohols.

Q: Why is acetic acid weaker than formic acid?

In acetic acid, due to the positive inductive effect (+I effect) of the methyl group, the electron density of the O–H bond increases, making it difficult to release the H+ ion.

In contrast, formic acid does not have this effect, so it ionises easily. Hence acetic acid is weaker than formic acid.

Q: Compare the acidic strength of acetic acid, formic acid, and chloroacetic acid.

The chlorine atom present in chloroacetic acid has a strong negative inductive effect (–I). Because of this, the electrons of the O–H bond are easily displaced towards oxygen and H+ is released easily. The CH3 group present in CH3COOH, which produces a (+I) effect, reduces its acidic character.

Formic acid has no group that produces a (+I) or (–I) effect. Hence formic acid is stronger than acetic acid, and chloroacetic acid is stronger than acetic acid. In short, chloroacetic acid is stronger than formic acid, and formic acid is stronger than acetic acid.

Order of acidic strength:

\[ ClCH_2COOH \;>\; HCOOH \;>\; CH_3COOH \]

The chemical properties of monocarboxylic acids are as follows.

(I) Substitution in the alkyl group

Hell-Volhard-Zelinsky reaction (HVZ): When a carboxylic acid is reacted with Cl₂ or Br₂ in the presence of phosphorus, an alpha-halogenated carboxylic acid is formed. This is called the Hell-Volhard-Zelinsky reaction (HVZ).

\[ CH_3COOH + Cl_2 \xrightarrow{P} CH_2ClCOOH + HCl \]

(II) Reactions due to the –COOH group

(A) Reactions in which the –COO–H bond breaks (formation of salts): Fatty acids dissolve in water to give hydrogen ions, so they form salts with metals, alkalis, and metal carbonates. For example —

1. Reaction with zinc — zinc acetate is formed.

\[ CH_3COOH + Zn \rightarrow (CH_3COO)_2Zn + H_2 \]

2. Reaction with NaOH — sodium acetate is formed.

\[ CH_3COOH + NaOH \rightarrow CH_3COONa + H_2O \] \[ C_6H_5COOH + NaOH \rightarrow C_6H_5COONa + H_2O \]

3. Reaction with Na₂CO₃ — sodium acetate is formed.

\[ 2CH_3COOH + Na_2CO_3 \rightarrow 2CH_3COONa + H_2O + CO_2 \]

(B) Reactions in which the –CO–OH bond breaks:

1. Reaction with alcohols (esterification) — heating with alcohols in the presence of dehydrating agents such as concentrated H₂SO₄ or dry HCl forms esters.

\[ CH_3COOH + C_2H_5OH \xrightarrow{\text{conc. } H_2SO_4} CH_3COOC_2H_5 + H_2O \] \[ C_6H_5COOH + C_2H_5OH \xrightarrow{\text{conc. } H_2SO_4} C_6H_5COOC_2H_5 + H_2O \]

2. Reaction with phosphorus trichloride — acid chlorides are formed.

\[ 3CH_3COOH + PCl_3 \rightarrow 3CH_3COCl + H_3PO_3 \] \[ 3C_6H_5COOH + PCl_3 \rightarrow 3C_6H_5COCl + H_3PO_3 \]

3. Reaction with phosphorus pentachloride — acid chlorides are formed.

\[ CH_3COOH + PCl_5 \rightarrow CH_3COCl + HCl + POCl_3 \] \[ C_6H_5COOH + PCl_5 \rightarrow C_6H_5COCl + HCl + POCl_3 \]

4. Reaction with thionyl chloride — acid chlorides are formed.

\[ CH_3COOH + SOCl_2 \rightarrow CH_3COCl + HCl + SO_2 \] \[ C_6H_5COOH + SOCl_2 \rightarrow C_6H_5COCl + HCl + SO_2 \]

5. Reaction with ammonia — ammonium salts are formed, which on heating give amides.

\[ CH_3COOH + NH_3 \rightarrow CH_3COONH_4 \xrightarrow{\Delta} CH_3CONH_2 + H_2O \] \[ C_6H_5COOH + NH_3 \rightarrow C_6H_5COONH_4 \xrightarrow{\Delta} C_6H_5CONH_2 + H_2O \]

6. Dehydration — when fatty acids are heated alone or with dehydrating agents such as phosphorus pentoxide, one molecule of water is removed and anhydrides are formed.

\[ 2CH_3COOH \xrightarrow{P_2O_5} (CH_3CO)_2O + H_2O \]

(C) Reactions in which the entire COOH group takes part:

1. Decarboxylation — when the anhydrous sodium salt of a fatty acid is heated with soda lime, alkanes are formed.

\[ CH_3COONa + NaOH \xrightarrow{CaO} CH_4 + Na_2CO_3 \] \[ C_6H_5COONa + NaOH \xrightarrow{CaO} C_6H_6 + Na_2CO_3 \]

2. Kolbe reaction — electrolysis of an aqueous solution of the sodium or potassium salts of fatty acids gives alkanes.

\[ 2CH_3COONa + 2H_2O \xrightarrow{\text{electrolysis}} CH_3\text{-}CH_3 + 2NaOH + 2CO_2 + H_2 \]
Note: In the source this equation was printed split across two lines and unclear (ending "...CH₃CH₃ + + NaOH + CO₂" followed on the next line by a lone "H₂"). It has been reconstructed above as the standard, balanced Kolbe electrolysis equation.

3. Schmidt reaction — when hydrazoic acid dissolved in an inert solvent such as CHCl₃ reacts with fatty acids in the presence of sulfuric acid at 50–55°C, primary amines are formed.

\[ CH_3COOH + HN_3 \xrightarrow{\text{conc. } H_2SO_4,\ 50\text{-}55^\circ C} CH_3NH_2 + CO_2 + N_2 \] \[ C_6H_5COOH + HN_3 \xrightarrow{\text{conc. } H_2SO_4,\ 50\text{-}55^\circ C} C_6H_5NH_2 + CO_2 + N_2 \]

4. Passing the vapours of fatty acids over heated MnO at 300°C — gives aldehydes and ketones.

\[ HCOOH + HCOOH \xrightarrow{MnO/300^\circ C} HCHO + CO_2 + H_2O \] \[ CH_3COOH + HCOOH \xrightarrow{MnO/300^\circ C} CH_3CHO + CO_2 + H_2O \] \[ CH_3COOH + CH_3COOH \xrightarrow{MnO/300^\circ C} CH_3COCH_3 + CO_2 + H_2O \]

5. Dry distillation of the calcium salt of a fatty acid — this gives aldehydes and ketones.

\[ (HCOO)_2Ca \xrightarrow{\text{dry distillation}} HCHO + CaCO_3 \] \[ (CH_3COO)_2Ca + (HCOO)_2Ca \xrightarrow{\text{dry distillation}} 2CH_3CHO + 2CaCO_3 \] \[ (CH_3COO)_2Ca \xrightarrow{\text{dry distillation}} CH_3COCH_3 + CaCO_3 \]

6. Reduction — fatty acids are reduced in the presence of lithium aluminium hydride to form alcohols.

\[ CH_3COOH + 4[H] \xrightarrow{LiAlH_4} CH_3CH_2OH + H_2O \]

7. Thermal decomposition of formic acid — when formic acid is heated to 160°C, it decomposes into CO and H₂O.

\[ HCOOH \xrightarrow{160^\circ C} CO + H_2O \]

Q: Although carboxylic acids have a C=O group, why do they not give addition reactions?

The C=O group of a carboxylic acid takes part in resonance. Hence it is stable, and because of this it does not give any addition reaction.

Q: What is vinegar? Write its uses.

A 6–10% dilute solution of acetic acid is called vinegar. Besides acetic acid, it also contains small amounts of some organic acids, esters, and colouring substances.

Uses — Vinegar is widely used in making pickles and whitewash.

Q: How is vinegar obtained?

Vinegar is generally obtained by fermentation of sugarcane juice or alcohol. This method is called the quick vinegar process.

Q: What is fermentation?

The process by which complex organic substances are converted into simpler organic substances by microorganisms such as bacteria and yeast is called fermentation.

Q: How would you prepare acetic acid by the quick vinegar process? Describe it based on the following points — (i) description of the method, (ii) diagram of the apparatus, (iii) equation of the reactions, (iv) precautions (any one).

Quick vinegar process — this is a method for the industrial production of acetic acid.

Principle: In this method, acetic acid (the main component of vinegar) is obtained from ethyl alcohol through fermentation by Mycoderma aceti bacteria.

Chemical equation:

\[ CH_3CH_2OH + O_2 \xrightarrow{\text{Mycoderma aceti bacteria}} CH_3COOH + CO_2 \]

Apparatus: A large wooden vat is used in this process, packed in the middle with wood shavings. These shavings are soaked in old vinegar so that they become inoculated with Mycoderma aceti bacteria. There is a perforated board in the upper part of the vat and a false perforated bottom below. Air is allowed to enter through holes made in the lower part of the vat.

Method: A dilute solution of ethanol (10–12%) is allowed to trickle slowly from the top of the vat downward. As the ethanol solution passes through the wood shavings in the middle, it comes into contact with Mycoderma aceti bacteria and air rising from below. The bacteria oxidise the ethanol to form acetic acid. The final product, vinegar, is collected from the bottom of the vat.

Precautions:

  1. An 8–10% ethyl alcohol solution is used.
  2. The system temperature is maintained at 25–35°C.
  3. Ammonium salt is added to the ethyl alcohol.
  4. Sufficient air is passed through.

Q: An organic compound with molecular formula C₉H₁₀O forms a 2,4-DNP derivative, reduces Tollens' reagent, and undergoes the Cannizzaro reaction. On strong oxidation, it gives 1,2-benzenedicarboxylic acid. Identify the compound.

Answer:

  1. Since the given compound, with molecular formula C₉H₁₀O, forms a 2,4-DNP derivative and reduces Tollens' reagent, it must be an aldehyde.
  2. Since it undergoes the Cannizzaro reaction, the –CHO group must be directly attached to the benzene ring (i.e., it has no α-hydrogen).
  3. Since it gives 1,2-benzenedicarboxylic acid on strong oxidation, it must be an ortho-substituted benzaldehyde. 2-Ethylbenzaldehyde is the only ortho-substituted aromatic aldehyde with molecular formula C₉H₁₀O.
