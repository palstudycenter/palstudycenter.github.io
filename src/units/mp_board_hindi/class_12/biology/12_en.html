📘 12-Ecosystem
Q: What is called an ecological system?
Answer:
An ecological system (Ecosystem) is a natural system in which all the living components (plants, animals and micro-organisms) and non-living components (water, air, soil, light, temperature, etc.) of an area interact with one another to form a balanced unit through the flow of energy and the cycling of nutrients.
Q: Write the functions of an ecological system.
Answer:

The main functions of an ecological system are as follows—

  1. Productivity: Green plants prepare organic food through photosynthesis, which supplies energy to all organisms.
  2. Energy Flow: Energy flows from the sun to producers, from producers to consumers, and finally to decomposers through food chains and food webs.
  3. Decomposition: Decomposers (bacteria and fungi) break down dead organisms and biological wastes, converting them into simpler substances.
  4. Nutrient Cycling: Nutrients such as carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, and water keep cycling continuously between the biotic and abiotic components.
  5. Maintaining ecological balance: It ensures the stability of the ecosystem and the continuity of life by maintaining balance between organisms and their environment.

Q: Write the components of an ecological system.
Answer:

An ecological system mainly has two types of components—

  1. Abiotic Components:

    These are the non-living components, such as— air, water, soil, light, temperature, humidity, and mineral salts, etc.

  2. Biotic Components:

    These are all the living components, which are divided into three parts—

    • Producers: Green plants, which make their own food through photosynthesis.
    • Consumers: Organisms that depend directly or indirectly on producers for food, such as— herbivores, carnivores, and omnivores.
    • Decomposers: Bacteria and fungi, which decompose dead organisms and biological remains and return nutrients to the environment.
Q: What are producers called? How many types are there?
Answer:

Producers: These are autotrophic organisms that make their own food from simple inorganic substances with the help of sunlight or chemical energy. In an ecological system, they form the first trophic level.

Producers are mainly of two types—

  1. Photosynthetic Producers: These prepare food through photosynthesis with the help of sunlight, such as— green plants, algae, and cyanobacteria.
  2. Chemosynthetic Producers: These prepare food using chemical energy obtained from the oxidation of inorganic substances, such as— nitrifying bacteria and sulphur bacteria.
Q: What are secondary producers called?
Answer:

Secondary Producers are organisms that do not make their own food but instead build their body tissues (biomass) by using food produced by primary producers. These are actually Consumers and they transfer energy from one trophic level to another in the ecological system.

Examples: Cow, goat, deer, rabbit, lion, tiger, human, etc.

Note: In modern ecology, the term "secondary producer" is used very rarely. Generally these are called Consumers.

Q: What is productivity called? How many types of it are there?
Answer:

Productivity is the rate at which producers (green plants) build organic matter (biomass) through photosynthesis in a given time and in a given area.

Productivity is mainly of two types—

  1. Gross Primary Productivity (GPP): This is the total organic matter that producers build through photosynthesis in a given time.
  2. Net Primary Productivity (NPP): This is the organic matter that remains after subtracting the matter used by producers in respiration. This is the energy and biomass available for consumers.

Formula:

Net Primary Productivity (NPP) = Gross Primary Productivity (GPP) − Respiration (R)

Q: What are consumers called? How many types are there?
Answer:

Consumers are heterotrophic organisms that cannot make their own food and depend directly or indirectly on producers or other organisms for food.

Consumers are mainly of four types—

  1. Primary Consumers: These feed directly on producers (green plants). They are also called Herbivores.
    Examples: Cow, goat, deer, rabbit, etc.
  2. Secondary Consumers: These feed on primary consumers. They are generally carnivores.
    Examples: Frog, lizard, snake, etc.
  3. Tertiary Consumers: These feed on secondary consumers and are higher-order carnivores.
    Examples: Hawk, lion, tiger, etc.
  4. Top Consumers: These are the final consumers in the food chain and have no natural predator.
    Examples: Human, lion, tiger, eagle, etc.
Q: What are decomposers called?
Answer:

Decomposers are micro-organisms that break down dead plants, animals, and biological wastes, converting them into simple inorganic substances. They maintain the balance of the ecological system by returning nutrients to the environment.

Examples: Bacteria and Fungi.

Q: Write the role of decomposers in an ecosystem.
Or
Write the functions of decomposers.
Answer:

The main roles of decomposers in an ecosystem are as follows—

  1. They decompose dead plants, animals, and biological wastes.
  2. They convert complex organic matter into simple inorganic substances.
  3. They return nutrients such as carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, etc., back to the soil and environment.
  4. They play an important role in Nutrient Cycling.
  5. They keep the environment clean and prevent the accumulation of dead remains.
  6. They help increase soil fertility and maintain ecological balance.
Q: What is decomposition called? Write the different stages of the decomposition process.
Answer:

Decomposition is the process in which decomposers convert dead organisms and biological remains into simple inorganic substances.

The main stages of the decomposition process are as follows—

  1. Fragmentation: Dead organic matter breaks into small pieces.
  2. Leaching: Water-soluble substances reach the deeper layers of the soil.
  3. Catabolism: Bacteria and fungi break down complex organic matter into simpler substances with the help of enzymes.
  4. Humification: As a result of decomposition, dark-coloured humus is formed, which increases the fertility of the soil.
  5. Mineralisation: Mineral elements are released from humus and other organic matter and mix back into the soil, where they are used by plants.
Q: What is called Humification?
Answer:

Humification is the process of decomposition in which the organic remains of dead plants and animals are partially decomposed to form Humus, a dark brown or black, complex, colloidal, and decay-resistant substance.

Q: What is Humus called? Write its characteristics.
Answer:

Humus is a dark brown or black complex organic substance found in soil, which is formed from the partial decomposition of dead organisms.

The characteristics of humus are as follows—

  1. It is dark brown or black in colour.
  2. It is a colloidal and decay-resistant substance.
  3. It increases the fertility and water-holding capacity of the soil.
  4. It helps in making essential nutrients available to plants.
  5. It improves the structure and aeration of the soil.
Q: How many types of Humus are there?
Answer:

Humus is mainly of two types—

  1. Mor Humus:

    This is found in acidic soils. In this, the rate of decomposition is slow and organic matter is not fully decomposed. It is generally found in coniferous forests.

  2. Mull Humus:

    This is found in fertile, neutral, or slightly alkaline soils. In this, decomposition occurs rapidly and the humus mixes well with the soil. It is generally found in grasslands and deciduous forests.

Q: What is called Mineralisation?
Answer:

Mineralisation is the process of decomposition in which decomposers convert humus and other organic matter into simple inorganic mineral substances (such as- nitrates, phosphates, sulphates, etc.). These mineral substances are again absorbed by plants.

Q: Write the factors affecting the process of decomposition.
Answer:

The process of decomposition is affected by the following factors—

  1. Temperature: Decomposition is fast at a suitable temperature.
  2. Humidity (Moisture): Adequate moisture increases the rate of decomposition.
  3. Oxygen: Availability of oxygen speeds up decomposition.
  4. Nature of the organic matter: Simple organic substances decompose quickly, while complex substances such as lignin and chitin decompose slowly.
  5. Number of micro-organisms: A higher number of bacteria and fungi increases the rate of decomposition.
Q: What are called Detritivores?
Answer:

Detritivores are organisms that feed on the remains (detritus) of dead plants and animals and break them into small pieces. They initiate the process of decomposition.

Examples: Earthworm, termite, dung beetle, etc.

Q: What are called Parasites?
Answer:

Parasites are organisms that live inside or on the body of another organism (host) and obtain food and shelter from it, while causing harm to the host in return.

Examples: Tapeworm, louse, dodder (Cuscuta), etc.

Q: What is a trophic level?
Or
Write the names of the trophic levels of an ecological system.
Answer:

Trophic Level is the position of organisms in a food chain, determined on the basis of how they obtain their food. Each trophic level is a step in the flow of energy.

The main trophic levels of an ecological system are as follows—

  1. First Trophic Level: Producers (green plants).
  2. Second Trophic Level: Primary consumers (herbivorous organisms).
  3. Third Trophic Level: Secondary consumers (primary carnivores).
  4. Fourth Trophic Level: Tertiary consumers (higher-order carnivores).
  5. Decomposers: Bacteria and fungi, which decompose dead organisms of all trophic levels and return nutrients to the environment.

Q: What is meant by Energy Flow?
Answer:

Energy Flow is the process in which energy flows in one direction from the sun to producers, from producers to various consumers, and finally to decomposers through food chains and food webs. This energy decreases progressively at each trophic level and does not return back to the sun.

Q: Write the law of energy flow in an ecological system.
Answer:

The main laws of energy flow in an ecological system are as follows—

  1. Energy flow is Unidirectional: Energy flows from the sun to producers, and from there to consumers and finally to decomposers. It does not return back to the sun or to the preceding trophic level.
  2. Energy is lost at each trophic level: At each level, most of the energy is lost as heat through respiration, metabolism, and other biological activities.
  3. Energy decreases progressively: The energy available at each subsequent trophic level keeps decreasing. Generally, only about 10% of the energy is transferred to the next trophic level, while the remaining 90% of the energy is lost.
Q: Write Lindeman's '10% Law'.
Answer:

According to Lindeman's 10% Law, only 10% of the total energy of any trophic level is transferred to the next trophic level, while the remaining 90% of the energy is lost as heat through respiration, metabolism, and other biological activities.

Example:

  • Producers = 1000 kcal
  • Primary consumers = 100 kcal
  • Secondary consumers = 10 kcal
  • Tertiary consumers = 1 kcal
Q: What is a food chain? Write its types found in nature.
Answer:

Food Chain is the sequential chain of organisms in which one organism becomes the food of another, and the flow of energy and nutrients takes place from producers to various consumers.

The food chain in nature is mainly of two types—

  1. Grazing Food Chain:

    This begins with green plants (producers) and reaches herbivores and then carnivores.

    Example:
    Grass → Grasshopper → Frog → Snake → Hawk

  2. Detritus Food Chain:

    This begins with the remains (detritus) of dead plants and animals. Detritivores and decomposers play the main role in it.

    Example:
    Dead leaves → Earthworm → Chicken → Hawk

Q: What is called a Food Web?
Answer:

Food Web is an interconnected network of several food chains in an ecological system. In it, an organism may be a part of more than one food chain, which creates many pathways for the flow of energy.

Importance:

  1. It maintains the stability of the ecological system.
  2. It keeps the food relationships between organisms balanced.
  3. If any one food chain is disrupted, the other chains maintain the flow of energy.

Q: What is an Ecological Pyramid called? How many types are there?
Answer:

Ecological Pyramid is a graphical representation in which the number, biomass, or energy of organisms at various trophic levels of an ecological system is shown in the form of a pyramid. In it, producers are located at the Base and higher consumers at the Top.

Ecological pyramids are mainly of three types—

  1. Pyramid of Numbers: This shows the number of organisms at each trophic level.
  2. Pyramid of Biomass: This shows the total biomass present at each trophic level.
  3. Pyramid of Energy: This shows the energy available at each trophic level. This pyramid is always Upright.
Q: What is called the Pyramid of Numbers?
Answer:

Pyramid of Numbers is an ecological pyramid in which the number of organisms present at various trophic levels of an ecological system is shown in the form of a pyramid.

The Pyramid of Numbers is of two types—

  1. Upright Pyramid of Numbers:

    In this, the number of producers is the highest and the number of organisms decreases progressively at each higher trophic level.

    Example: Grass → Deer → Lion

  2. Inverted Pyramid of Numbers:

    In this, the number of producers is low, while the number of consumers and parasites dependent on them can be higher.

    Example: One tree → Many birds/insects → Countless parasites

Q: What is called the Pyramid of Biomass?
Answer:

Pyramid of Biomass is an ecological pyramid in which the total living weight (Biomass) of organisms present at various trophic levels of an ecological system is shown in the form of a pyramid. Biomass means the total dry weight of organisms present per unit area at a given time.

The Pyramid of Biomass is of two types—

  1. Upright Pyramid of Biomass:

    In this, the biomass of producers is the highest and the biomass decreases progressively at each higher trophic level.

    Example: Grassland and forest ecosystems.

  2. Inverted Pyramid of Biomass:

    In this, the biomass of producers is lower compared to that of consumers.

    Example: Aquatic ecosystems, where the biomass of phytoplankton is low and the biomass of zooplankton is relatively higher.

Q: What is called the Pyramid of Energy?
Answer:

Pyramid of Energy is an ecological pyramid in which the amount of energy available at various trophic levels of an ecological system is shown in the form of a pyramid.

In this pyramid, producers have the highest energy, and the energy decreases progressively at each subsequent trophic level, because most of the energy is lost as heat through respiration and other biological activities.

Characteristic: The Pyramid of Energy is always Upright, because the flow of energy is unidirectional and only about 10% of the energy at each trophic level is transferred to the next level.

Q: Why is the Pyramid of Energy always upright?
Answer:

The Pyramid of Energy is always Upright, because at each trophic level, most of the energy is lost as heat through respiration, metabolism, and other biological activities. As a result, only about 10% of the energy reaches the next trophic level.

For this reason, energy is the highest in producers and decreases progressively at each higher trophic level. Hence the Pyramid of Energy is never inverted.
